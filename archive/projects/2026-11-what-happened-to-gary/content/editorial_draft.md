# What Happened to Gary?
*Once a perfectly ordinary name for a British baby boy. Now something of a rarity.*

In 1964, Gary was the 16th most popular boys' name in England and Wales.

Not a particularly unusual choice. Not an ironic one. Just Gary.

Six decades later, Gary hasn't disappeared from Britain. He's simply stopped turning up on birth certificates.

## Gary's moment

The historical rankings tell the first part of the story. Gary was 95th in 1944, 34th in 1954 and 16th in 1964. He was still 26th in 1984, but had slipped out of the top 100 by 1994.

One possible clue comes from Hollywood. Gary Cooper was born *Frank James Cooper*. He adopted Gary as a stage name in 1925, reportedly taking inspiration from Gary, Indiana. He became a major film star, and name historians link him to Gary's growing popularity. Could his fame have helped the name cross the Atlantic?

Possibly. But the historical British figures are rankings at ten-year intervals, not annual registrations. They cannot tell us which film, celebrity or event made parents choose Gary.

## A rather long goodbye

The annual records take over in 1996. That year, 235 baby boys were registered as Gary in England and Wales.

By 2024, that number was **eight**. A **96.6% decline** in registrations.

Not eight thousand. Eight.

And this was hardly a celebrity-free period. British culture had Gary Lineker, Gary Barlow and Gary Oldman. Their popularity as public figures did not prevent the name's downward trajectory. That doesn't prove celebrity had no influence, either: the comparable annual series starts in 1996, too late to capture the beginning of Take That's success or Lineker's 1986 World Cup heroics.

Gary isn't the only name slipping away. Among boys born in England and Wales, Nigel fell from 31 registrations in 1996 to five in 2024; Wayne from 84 to 12; Barry from 45 to six. It's less a mystery about one name than a change in how generations decide what sounds right for a newborn.

## Meanwhile, Arthur is having a lovely time

Here is the twist. In 1996, **129** baby boys were named Arthur. By 2024 the count was **3,368**.

Gary went from 235 to eight. Arthur went from 129 to 3,368.

Same country. Same years. Entirely different trajectories.

Perhaps Arthur now sounds timeless while Gary sounds tied to a particular generation. That's a plausible cultural interpretation, not a conclusion the birth records can establish.

**Chart 3: Arthur came back. Gary didn't.** — directly comparable annual registrations on one scale.

There's something reassuring about the whole thing. Names don't really die. They linger in families, workplaces, school reunions and phone contact lists. They just stop being chosen for the next generation.

**Gary hasn't vanished. He simply isn't getting many younger namesakes.**

## TL;DR

Gary climbed to 16th place in the 1964 boys' naming rankings in England and Wales. In the annual records, registrations fell from **235 in 1996 to eight in 2024**. Arthur moved the other way: **129 to 3,368**. Fame may help a name arrive, but it doesn't guarantee that it stays fashionable.
