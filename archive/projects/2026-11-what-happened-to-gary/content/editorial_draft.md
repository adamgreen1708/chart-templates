# What Happened to Gary?
*Once one of Britain's perfectly ordinary boys' names. Now an increasingly rare sight on a birth certificate.*

At some point, Gary stopped sounding like a baby and started sounding like someone who knows where the spare extension lead is kept.

But Gary wasn't always a name you associated with a colleague, an uncle, or somebody who's been working in accounts since the fax machine was cutting-edge.

In **1964, Gary was the 16th most popular boys' name in England and Wales**.

Not an eccentric choice. Not a throwback. Just Gary.

## Gary's big moment

Back in 1944, Gary was 95th in the boys' top 100. By 1954 he'd reached 34th, and in 1964 he was 16th. Gary was having a very respectable decade.

Hollywood offers one possible clue. **Gary Cooper was actually born Frank James Cooper**. His adopted stage name became famous long before Britain's Gary boom; the actor is widely credited with helping popularise the name in America.

Did British parents follow suit? Possibly. But the historical figures give us **rankings only, every ten years**, not actual birth counts. They cannot tell us precisely when the trend took off or who deserves the credit.

## A long goodbye. With quite a few famous Garys along the way.

The annual counts pick up in 1996. That year, **235** newborn boys were registered as Gary.

By 2024: **eight**.

Not eight hundred. Eight. That's a **96.6% drop**.

This wasn't for lack of publicity. We had **Gary Lineker** in football, **Gary Barlow** in pop and **Gary Oldman** in films. There was no shortage of well-known Garys. The shortage was at the maternity ward.

In 2015, [Fay Schopen wrote in *The Guardian*](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2015/mar/11/gary-not-worried-say-goodbye-garys) about the name's decline. The article even recalled a satirical prediction of a Gary-free Britain by 2050 — a joke that had been taken rather more seriously than intended.

The Gary she interviewed wasn't concerned. Being one of relatively few Garys had its advantages: people remembered him.

The numbers have continued their downward journey. There were **28 baby Garys in 2013** and just **eight in 2024**.

Gary has company, too. Between 1996 and 2024, Nigel fell from 31 registrations to five; Wayne from 84 to 12; and Barry from 45 to six. It's not just one name quietly slipping out of fashion.

**Chart 2: Very few baby Garys left** — annual registrations, 1996–2024.

## Meanwhile, Arthur is having a lovely time

And then there's Arthur.

In 1996, **129** boys were named Arthur. By 2024, it was **3,368**.

While Gary headed towards the exit, Arthur came swaggering back through the nursery door. Same country. Same years. Two very different careers.

Perhaps Arthur sounds charmingly old-fashioned, while Gary still reminds today's parents of their dad's mate who can get a good deal on tyres. That's a theory about generational taste, not something birth registrations can prove.

**Chart 3: Arthur came back. Gary didn't.** — annual registrations on the same scale.

There is a final twist here. Garys themselves aren't disappearing; those born decades ago are very much still around. It's the *new* Garys who have become a rarity.

Perhaps one day the fashion will turn, and Gary will make the sort of comeback Arthur has enjoyed.

If it does, a new generation of parents will be able to say they discovered Gary before it was cool.

**Until then, Britain isn't running out of Garys. It's running out of baby Garys.**

## TL;DR

Gary reached **No. 16 in 1964** in England and Wales. Its annual baby registrations fell from **235 in 1996 to eight in 2024** — despite a healthy supply of famous Garys. Arthur, meanwhile, went from **129 to 3,368**. Naming fashions are strange. Gary deserves a comeback.
