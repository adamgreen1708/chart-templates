# Can you find the four outsiders?

Berlin, 2005. Mizuki Noguchi wins the women's marathon. That's six straight victories for Japan in this race, dating back to 2000. Six years, six Japanese champions. And a rather different picture from the one we associate with marathon running today.

## The world still won

Look across six big-city marathons in the 2000s. Every dot is a women's race winner, with a two-letter country code inside. Grey is Kenya or Ethiopia. Red means somewhere else.

*[Chart 1 — Berlin: six straight for Japan]*

There's plenty of red. **31 of 53 victories** went to other countries, including eight for Japan and seven for the United Kingdom. Berlin's first six dots are all **JP**. Quite a run, literally.

But take the same races and move the calendar forward ten years.

## Now find the four

Same six race rows. Same colours, same country codes. One dot for every completed race. How many red dots can you spot?

*[Chart 2 — Find the four red dots]*

**Four. Out of 59.** Two at Tokyo — Alevtina Biktimirova (Russia, 2010) and Noriko Higuchi (Japan, 2011) — then Shalane Flanagan (New York, 2017) and Desiree Linden (Boston, 2018), both from the US.

Higuchi was [awarded the 2011 title](https://www.marathon.tokyo/en/about/past/2011/) after the original winner's disqualification. And that blank New York spot in 2012? [Hurricane Sandy cancelled the race](https://worldathletics.org/news/news/new-york-marathon-cancelled). Neither is a missing value dressed up as a result.

## The bigger shift wasn't the men's

The next chart steps back to compare men and women across the two decades. The measure is the share of wins **outside Kenya and Ethiopia** — not the share of runners taking part.

*[Chart 3 — The big shift wasn't the men's]*

For women, that share fell from **58.5% to 6.8%**. A drop of **51.7 percentage points**. For men it went from **22.6% to 10.2%** — just **12.5 points**. Men's winning nationalities were concentrated already. Women's changed much more dramatically.

## So where did the red go?

The data tells us *what* happened, not *why*. But there are a few clues. Historian [Michelle Sikes](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/qa-kenyas-trailblazing-women-runners-chart-course-success-new-book) describes how Kenyan women gained more access to professional racing after facing barriers that men didn't face to the same degree.

Then there's the depth of competition. [World Athletics](https://worldathletics.org/news/news/2010-end-of-year-reviews-road-runningrace) counted **18 Ethiopian women** under 2:26 for a marathon in 2010, up from eight the year before. By [2011](https://worldathletics.org/news/news/2011-end-of-year-reviews-road-runningrace), Kenyan and Ethiopian women occupied **16 of the world's top 20** marathon rankings. Meanwhile, Japan was working out how to follow an exceptional generation of champions.

None of that proves what caused these particular results. We'd need starting fields and a lot more besides. But the possibility is intriguing: **the winners' map may have narrowed partly because more women had a route into world-class racing.**

## TLDR

Six straight Japanese women's wins at Berlin became just **four non-Kenyan/non-Ethiopian women's wins** across six big-city marathons in the 2010s. The shift was much bigger for women than men. Wider opportunity and a deeper elite field may help explain it, but the charts alone can't prove why.

### A note on the numbers

The three charts use the pinned [Shorts in Sports winners dataset](https://github.com/shortsinsports/Data/blob/4ea0ca9e403d706a734d41088ad63e571ab0ca21/SIS-marathon_major_winners_2000_2026.csv) for **six historical major races**, not all today's World Marathon Majors. The comparison covers **53 completed race-years in 2000–09 and 59 in 2010–19**. Tokyo enters in 2007; cancelled New York 2012 is excluded. Country codes are ISO 3166-1 alpha-2, so the UK is **GB**.

The [GitHub data QA](https://github.com/adamgreen1708/chart-templates/blob/main/archive/projects/2026-11-marathon-winning-map/data/qa_report.json) checks the records and calculations and verifies explicitly flagged results. It is **not a complete official-results audit**: 216 completed source entries remain labelled *Not independently checked*. This is a story about winning nationalities in the supplied races, not all competitors or the causes of success.
