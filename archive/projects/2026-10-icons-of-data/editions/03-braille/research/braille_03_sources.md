# Edition 03 — Historical and encoding source audit
**Researched:** 9 October 2026
**Status:** UK UEB example audit completed against RNIB/UKAAF/ICEB; historical photo licence and publication QA still outstanding.

## Authoritative scope: UK English (not an American legacy code)
- **UK code:** Unified English Braille (**UEB**), adopted by the UK Association for Accessible Formats (**UKAAF**) in 2011; UK transition from Standard English Braille (**SEB**) completed in 2015, according to RNIB.
- **UK primary sources:** Royal National Institute of Blind People (**RNIB**) for the six-dot cell, alphabet/number principles, 63 raised patterns and UK implementation; UKAAF for its UK adoption and standards references.
- **Normative code rules:** International Council on English Braille (**ICEB**), *Rules of Unified English Braille*, Third Edition 2024, as endorsed by UKAAF and sold/referenced by RNIB.
- **Important nuance:** UEB is a **shared** international English braille code. US users of UEB do not use a different dot-1 sign for `a` or a different dots-3456 number indicator. Older **English Braille American Edition (EBAE)** and older UK **Standard English Braille (SEB)** did have differences; we do not use either legacy code as the visual reference.
- **Historical source boundary:** RNIB and Perkins may be cited for Louis Braille's nineteenth-century life and 1829 book, but never treat early French symbols as automatically identical to today's UEB examples.

## Matters for careful handling
- 'Six dots, 64 patterns' can be technically true including blank; RNIB describes **63 raised-dot combinations**, plus an empty cell. Use precise wording.
- Use UK spelling, UK accessibility references, and UEB rather than references to US legacy braille; historical US institutions may be retained **only for independent biographical evidence**.
- Colour must never imply the real tactile code depends on hue; use colour only as a visual teaching highlight.
- Images should convey the dot positions to sighted learners *and* provide equivalent written dot positions for screen readers; they can't simulate touch.
- Keep claims about innovation/freedom grounded in the documented reading/writing affordances of early braille, not generalized claims about all blind experiences.
- The blog's audience likes a dry tone, but jokes should target our visualisation assumptions, never blind readers.
