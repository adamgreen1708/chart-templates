# How the world changed colour

*Icons of Data · Edition 07 · Ed Hawkins' Warming Stripes (2018)*

**Working manuscript — GitHub-rendered review images available; not published.**

What if a chart lost its axis, its labels and every last number?

Normally, that sounds like a design review going rather badly.

In 2018, climate scientist Ed Hawkins made it work.

## One year. One stripe.

His *Warming Stripes* turn annual temperatures into a row of coloured bands. Cooler-than-average years are blue; warmer-than-average years move towards red. There is no line to follow and no degree symbol to decipher.

Read from left to right and the colours change.

The message does not need much explaining.

The stripes were developed at the University of Reading, with an early version made for the Hay Festival in 2018. Later versions showed the global temperature record stretching back to 1850.

*[Visual 1 — The actual, unframed University of Reading *Global 2020* artwork, UAC/11018 (1850–2020), with title and attribution in the article caption.]*

## The chart with almost nothing on it

There is a clever decision hiding inside all that simplicity.

Each band gets exactly the same amount of horizontal space: one year. Its colour carries the temperature information. The order carries the passage of time.

You do not have to know much about charts to spot that the right-hand side has become much redder.

It is an argument made with remarkably few ingredients.

*[Visual 2 — New data-faithful reconstruction from verified annual global HadCRUT5 series, with a tiny conventional temperature view underneath. Separate sources and baselines explicitly labelled.]*

But what makes it so immediate also introduces a trade-off.

Even the midpoint of the colours has a history. When Hawkins updated the stripes for 2023, he shifted their reference average from 1971–2000 to 1961–2010 and widened the colour range to accommodate the unusually hot new observations. There is no special temperature event in 1961: it is simply the first year of the chosen averaging period.

## Beautifully clear. Deliberately incomplete.

The stripes do not tell us the exact temperature for a particular year. They hide the degree scale, omit uncertainty and depend on a chosen reference period and colour range.

And blue is not a verdict that a historical year was 'cold' everywhere. These are global annual averages, not local weather forecasts.

The colour also needs a legend or explanation for readers who cannot reliably distinguish the shades.

*[Visual 3 — Decode the Icon: annual time slots; colour encoding; baseline and colour-range choices; what the missing axis and uncertainty leave out.]*

## TLDR

Hawkins did not make temperature data less serious.

He made the first encounter with it less demanding.

A wonderful way to start a conversation.

Not quite the last word in it.
