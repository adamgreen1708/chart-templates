# Dr Seuss word invention — research plan

## Core question

What makes a made-up Dr Seuss word feel recognisably Seussian rather than simply random?

The strongest route is linguistic rather than bibliographic: use published quantitative analysis of Seuss's invented words to show that the apparent nonsense has recurring grammatical, sound and letter-level patterns.

## Primary quantitative source

Daniel Teuber (2018), *An Analysis of the Nonce-Words of Dr. Seuss*, Journal of Osaka Sangyo University Humanities & Social Sciences 34, pp. 43–69.

The paper builds a lexicon of **377 type-2 nonce words from 33 Dr Seuss books**. The author's definition deliberately focuses on words whose meaning depends on context and excludes many ordinary compounds or transparent formations.

This project uses only aggregate measures published in the study. It does not reproduce the books or store a full copyrighted text corpus.

## Measures used

### Parts of speech

Published rounded shares:

- common noun — 61%
- proper noun — 27%
- adjective — 5%
- interjection — 4%
- verb — 3%

The editorial observation is that common and proper nouns together make up **88%** of the published distribution.

### Rhyme and alliteration

Published Figure 4 shares:

- rhyme only — 48%
- rhyme + alliteration — 19%
- alliteration only — 17%
- neither — 16%

The first three categories sum to **84%**, so roughly five-sixths of the sampled coinages show rhyme and/or alliteration influence.

### Letter Z

The paper's letter-frequency comparison reports:

- Seuss nonce-word sample — Z = 4.42% of letters; 377 words and 2,556 letters.
- top 1,000 US surnames comparison — Z = 1.02%.
- 3,000 common English words comparison — Z = 0.10%.

The derived Seuss/common-word ratio is **44.2×**.

The chart must describe this as a comparison of **letter shares**. It must not imply equal sample sizes or compare raw Z counts.

## Independent corroboration

Bruce Hayes (2022), *The coinages in Seuss*, English Language & Linguistics, Cambridge University Press, independently treats Seuss's coinages as systematic rather than arbitrary. The analysis identifies recurring phonological principles and reports initial /z/ as the most frequent phonestheme in that dataset.

This is useful corroboration for the editorial interpretation, but the chart values remain sourced consistently from Teuber (2018).

## Official contextual background

The Seussville author timeline records the constrained-vocabulary origin of *The Cat in the Hat* and the 50-word challenge behind *Green Eggs and Ham*. That context is useful for eventual blog copy because it shows deliberate language constraint elsewhere in Seuss's practice.

It is not used as a quantitative input to the three charts.

## Scope cautions

- Teuber's 377-word list is a defined linguistic sample, not every coined or unusual Seuss word.
- The percentages for parts of speech and sound influence are published as rounded whole percentages.
- The letter-frequency comparison sets differ in size and construction.
- “Seussian” is an editorial description of recurring patterns, not a formal statistical classification.
- No claim should be made that Z alone explains Seuss's style.

## Recommended analytical route

1. establish what kinds of invented words dominate;
2. show how strongly rhyme/alliteration structure the sample;
3. land on Z as a memorable letter-level signature;
4. keep the limitations visible so the charts describe the study rather than over-generalising to the complete oeuvre.
