# Publication package — Simon Reeve's Tropic of Capricorn

## Working headline

One invisible line. 23,000 miles of detours.

## Standfirst

Simon Reeve followed the Tropic of Capricorn through 10 countries and three continents. The line is straight. The journey was anything but.

## Editorial opening

A line on a globe looks wonderfully simple.

The Tropic of Capricorn is not.

Simon Reeve's journey followed the southern edge of the tropics eastwards for around 23,000 miles, starting on the Atlantic coast of Namibia and eventually reaching the Atlantic again in Brazil. In between came Botswana, South Africa, Mozambique, Madagascar, Australia, Chile, Argentina and Paraguay.

That makes the route easy to list — and rather harder to imagine.

## Reeve before the route

Reeve's path into travel television was not especially direct either. His official biography describes leaving school without qualifications, time on the dole, work in charity shops and supermarkets, then a newspaper post-boy job before moving into investigative journalism.

Before the long run of BBC journeys, he wrote current-affairs books about subjects including terrorism, organised crime and nuclear smuggling. That background helps explain why his travel programmes tend to use geography as the way into a story rather than treating scenery as the whole story.

## Chart 1 — Following the invisible line

The simplest version of the journey is:

**Namibia → Botswana → South Africa → Mozambique → Madagascar → Australia → Chile → Argentina → Paraguay → Brazil**

The opening chart deliberately ignores physical distance. Ten evenly spaced stops make the sequence readable, while the zig-zag around 23.4° south establishes the central idea: the Tropic is the thread, not the road.

## Chart 2 — The Tropic was a guide, not a road

Putting named stops roughly by longitude makes the route more revealing.

Across Africa, Australia and South America, Reeve repeatedly moves north and south of the Tropic rather than tracing it precisely. The chart exaggerates the vertical scale 3× to make those deviations legible and uses separate longitude panels so the enormous ocean crossings do not crush the detail.

This is a schematic analytical view, not a GPS reconstruction.

## Chart 3 — Eight ways to follow a straight line

Reeve's own account names eight ways of travelling: van, car, train, road train, helicopter, plane, boat and horseback.

Grouped simply by how they move, that becomes four ground modes, two air modes, one water mode and one animal mode.

Even the transport refuses to behave like a straight line.

## TL;DR

A roughly 23.4° south line became a 23,000-mile journey through 10 countries, three continents and eight named forms of transport.

The simplest lines on a map can produce wonderfully messy journeys in real life.
