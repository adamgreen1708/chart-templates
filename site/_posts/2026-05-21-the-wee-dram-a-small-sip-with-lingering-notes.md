<p>I’ve never really been a fan of heavily smoky and peaty whisky.</p>

<p>For years, mention Scotch and I immediately thought of medicinal, smoky drams that taste like bonfires and sea spray 🥴🤢. Turns out that may have just been my dads drinks cupboard!</p>

<p>After a few well organised whisky tasting sessions with my bro, we started digging into the flavours we liked and the distilleries behind them. I realised that stereotype only tells a very small part of 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁳󠁣󠁴󠁿’s story, along with the discovery that I sit on the polar opposite spectrum to my brothers taste preferences.</p>

<p>This post attempts to visually map what else Scotland’s whisky regions have to offer, a more informed trip to the supermarket and that kid in a sweet shop trip to the bar on a Saturday session 🍭</p>

<hr />

<h2>How the flavour maps work</h2>

<p>The charts in this post use flavour scores from the <a href="https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/koki25ando/scotch-whisky-dataset">Kaggle Scotch Whisky Flavour Dataset</a> Covering over 80 distilleries across Scotlands six whisky regions. The dataset rates distilleries across flavour dimensions like sweetness, fruitiness, smokiness and medicinal intensity.</p>

<p>To simplify things, I combined those flavours into two custom indexes:</p>

<ul>
<li>The 👎🏻(<em>i don’t t like it</em>) <strong>Peat index</strong> = Smoky + Medicinal</li>

<li>And the 👍🏻(<em>I like it</em>) <strong>Approachable index</strong> = Sweetness + Fruity + Honey</li>
</ul>

<p>I also created a simple <strong>“peat avoider score”</strong>, which rewards sweeter and fruitier whiskies while penalising smoky and medicinal flavours. It’s not meant to be scientific, just a useful way to visualise which distilleries sit furthest from the classic <em>peat-heavy</em> stereotype.</p>

<hr />

<h2>Scotch is sweeter than its reputation</h2>

<p>Across Scotland as a whole, sweet, fruity and honeyed notes appear far more often than extreme smoky flavours.</p>

<p>Whilst stereotype exists, the average Scotch profile actually sits much closer to <em>approachable</em> than <em>overpowering</em>.</p>

<hr />

<h2>Peat is whisky’s loud minority</h2>

<p>Smoke dominates whisky conversations because it’s memorable, distinctive and polarising. But in the flavour data, peat-heavy whiskies are actually a minority rather than the norm.</p>

<p>Most Scotch distilleries sit well away from the extreme smoky corner occupied by brands like Ardbeg, Laphroaig and Lagavulin.</p>

<figure class="story-chart full-bleed"><img src="/assets/migrated/the-wee-dram-a-small-sip-with-lingering-notes/img_8028.png" alt="IMG 8028" loading="lazy"><figcaption>% of distilleries by “smoky” score 1-4</figcaption></figure>

<hr />

<h2>Islay is the outlier, not the standard</h2>

<p>Once you split Scotland into regions, the story becomes much clearer.</p>

<p>Islay really does sit in its own smoky and medicinal corner of the map, driven by distilleries like Laphroaig, Ardbeg, Lagavulin and Caol Ila.</p>

<p>But that’s exactly the point: Islay is unusual.</p>

<p>It’s probably the loudest regional flavour profile in Scotch, but it’s far from representative of Scotland overall.</p>

<figure class="story-chart full-bleed"><img src="/assets/migrated/the-wee-dram-a-small-sip-with-lingering-notes/img_8032-1.png" alt="IMG 8032" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Average flavour score 1-4 by tasting note, highlighting Islay distilleries </figcaption></figure>

<hr />

<h2>A whisky map for peat avoiders</h2>

<p>For someone like me who prefers sweeter and fruitier whisky styles, the regional flavour maps become surprisingly useful.</p>

<p>Speyside — home to names like Macallan, Glenfiddich, Aberlour and Balvenie — clusters heavily around approachable profiles.</p>

<p>The Highlands cover almost the entire flavour spectrum, with distilleries like Dalmore, Oban and Glenmorangie stretching across the map.</p>

<p>The Islands sit somewhere in the middle with Talisker and Highland Park balancing smoke with sweeter notes, while Lowlands remain consistently lighter and softer with distilleries like Auchentoshan and Glenkinchie.</p>

<p>Even tiny Campbeltown lands almost exactly on the boundary between peat and approachability through Springbank and Glen Scotia.</p>

<p>Instead of treating Scotch as one category, the regional maps start to behave more like a flavour guide.</p>

<figure class="story-chart full-bleed"><img src="/assets/migrated/the-wee-dram-a-small-sip-with-lingering-notes/img_8034-1.png" alt="IMG 8034" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Ranked Peat avoider score by distillery, Islay region highlight </figcaption></figure>

<hr />

<h2>Scotland’s whisky regions tell very different stories</h2>

<p>Viewed together, the six regional maps completely change the picture.</p>

<p>Rather than one national flavour identity, Scotland looks more like a collection of regional flavour ecosystems — each with its own balance between sweetness, fruit, smoke and medicinal intensity.</p>

<p>And for peat avoiders like me, that’s actually great news.</p>

<h2>TL;DR</h2>

<p>Scotch whisky’s smoky reputation is largely driven by a small number of peat-heavy Islay distilleries. But flavour data from 86 Scottish distilleries shows most Scotch actually leans sweeter, fruitier and more approachable. By mapping Scotland’s whisky regions, a much broader flavour landscape emerges — from Speyside sweetness to Highland diversity and Island balance — proving peat is whisky’s loud minority, not its default flavour.<br></p>
