Berlin, 2005. Mizuki Noguchi crosses the line to win the marathon.

That's **six consecutive Japanese women's victories in Berlin**, stretching back to 2000.

Six years. Six Japanese winners.

It's not quite the picture of marathon dominance I expected to find.

## The world still won

Look across six big-city marathons — Tokyo, Boston, London, Berlin, Chicago and New York — in the 2000s.

Kenya and Ethiopia were already important parts of the picture. But the winners in this dataset came from **ten nationalities**.

Japanese women won eight times. British women won seven. Russia and Germany had their moments, too.

In the first chart, one dot means one women's race winner. **Red means the winning nationality was outside Kenya or Ethiopia.**

![Women's winners at six city marathons from 2000 to 2009: 31 of 53 dots are red for nationalities outside Kenya and Ethiopia, including Berlin's consecutive Japanese winners.](/assets/charts/marathon-winning-map/01_berlin_six_and_the_world.png){: .ctv-centred-chart }

The red dots aren't especially hard to find.

**31 of 53 women's victories** went to countries outside Kenya and Ethiopia.

And look at Berlin's row. The first six dots belong to Japanese winners — a consecutive run confirmed in [Berlin's historical results](https://www.bmw-berlin-marathon.com/fileadmin/media/events/berlinmarathon/fotos/Presse/Mediaguide/Statistiken/BM25AlleSiegerInnenBERLIN-MARATHON.pdf).

Now keep that chart in mind.

## Can you find the four?

Fast-forward to 2010–19.

The next chart uses **the same six race rows, the same decade-wide layout and the same colours**. One dot, one winner. The red still means someone from outside Kenya or Ethiopia.

**How many red dots can you spot?**

![The same six city marathons from 2010 to 2019, with four red dots among 59 women's winners. The red exceptions occur at Tokyo in 2010 and 2011, New York in 2017 and Boston in 2018.](/assets/charts/marathon-winning-map/02_find_the_four.png){: .ctv-centred-chart }

There are only **four red dots among 59 completed races**.

They belong to **Alevtina Biktimirova** (Tokyo 2010, Russia), **Noriko Higuchi** (Tokyo 2011, Japan), **Shalane Flanagan** (New York 2017, United States) and **Desiree Linden** (Boston 2018, United States).

There's an interesting wrinkle in Higuchi's case. The original 2011 Tokyo winner was disqualified, so [Tokyo's official record](https://www.marathon.tokyo/en/about/past/2011/) recognises Higuchi as champion. Excluding everything with a disqualification note would have made the chart wrong.

The New York gap in 2012 has a different explanation: the race was [cancelled after Hurricane Sandy](https://worldathletics.org/news/news/new-york-marathon-cancelled). It isn't a missing winner that we've quietly counted as zero.

And the red has almost disappeared.

But there's another twist.

## The bigger shift wasn't in the men's race

Men's winning nationalities were already concentrated in the 2000s. Only **12 of 53** men's winners in these races came from outside Kenya and Ethiopia.

For women, the equivalent figure was **31 of 53**.

In the following decade, those figures were **six of 59 men** and **four of 59 women**.

The contrast is clearer when both are plotted on the same percentage scale.

![The share of marathon victories from outside Kenya and Ethiopia in the 2000s versus 2010s: women's share falls from 58.5 to 6.8 percent, while men's falls from 22.6 to 10.2 percent. The y-axis starts at zero.](/assets/charts/marathon-winning-map/03_women_changed_most.png){: .ctv-centred-chart }

The women's share fell from **58.5% to 6.8%** — a **51.7 percentage-point drop**.

The men's fell from **22.6% to 10.2%** — **12.5 points**.

The men's map was narrow to begin with. The women's changed dramatically.

It's the sort of difference you might never notice if you only compared today's winners, or only looked at the fastest times.

## So why did the red dots disappear?

The charts show **what** changed. They don't tell us **why**. But looking beyond the dataset reveals a more interesting story than one country simply getting faster.

**First, the route into elite racing widened for women.** Historian [Michelle Sikes](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/qa-kenyas-trailblazing-women-runners-chart-course-success-new-book) describes how Kenyan women historically faced more barriers to international competition than men. As distance running became a paid profession in the late 1980s and 1990s, more women were able to pursue it as a career. The men's success had a substantial head start.

**Then came strength in numbers.** In its [2010 season review](https://worldathletics.org/news/news/2010-end-of-year-reviews-road-runningrace), World Athletics counted **18 Ethiopian women** running a marathon in under 2 hours 26 minutes, up from **eight in 2009**. In [2011](https://worldathletics.org/news/news/2011-end-of-year-reviews-road-runningrace), Kenya and Ethiopia supplied **16 of the world's top 20 women marathon runners**. Those are global performance figures, not results from our six selected races, but they suggest a much deeper pool of women capable of challenging for wins.

**Meanwhile, Japan's golden generation was changing.** By 2010, [World Athletics was asking](https://worldathletics.org/news/news/2010-end-of-year-reviews-road-runningrace) who would follow the likes of Naoko Takahashi and Mizuki Noguchi at the top of international marathons. That doesn't mean Japanese women stopped running. It means the remarkable generation behind those early Berlin dots wasn't so easily replaced.

Altitude, training, running economy and economic opportunity may all matter. A [review of Kenyan and Ethiopian running research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22634972/) found no single genetic or physiological explanation for their success. The bigger picture is more complicated—and more human.

We can't assign a percentage of this change to any one cause. To do that we'd need the starting fields, finishing times and access to elite competition over the years, not just the winners.

But here's the intriguing possibility: **the map of winners may have narrowed partly because the pathway into world-class racing was opening up for more women.**

## TLDR

**Six straight Japanese women's wins in Berlin. A decade later, just four winners from outside Kenya and Ethiopia across our six marathons. The women's winning-nationality mix changed far more sharply than the men's.**

The wider research suggests a combination of **greater professional opportunity, a deeper East African women's talent pool and a changing Japanese elite generation**. Our charts show the shift; they cannot prove exactly what caused it.

### A note on the numbers

These charts are based on the [Shorts in Sports marathon winners dataset](https://github.com/shortsinsports/Data/blob/4ea0ca9e403d706a734d41088ad63e571ab0ca21/SIS-marathon_major_winners_2000_2026.csv), processed and reproduced in [Coffeetableviz's chart-templates repository](https://github.com/adamgreen1708/chart-templates/tree/main/archive/projects/2026-11-marathon-winning-map). The sample covers **six historical major marathons**, not every present-day World Marathon Major.

The comparison uses **112 completed, matched race-years** in the supplied file: **53 in 2000–09 and 59 in 2010–19**. Both men's and women's results are included for each race-year. Tokyo enters the sample only in 2007; cancelled races, notably New York 2012, are excluded. Nationality is the source's recorded nationality of the **winner**, not the birthplace of athletes or the nationality of everyone who ran.

**Important limitation:** this is a **source-derived analysis, not a fully independently verified official-results database**. The repository's checks establish row and calculation integrity, and the eight explicitly flagged source entries and highlighted exceptions were compared with organisers' results. However, **216 completed source entries** remain classified *Not independently checked* in the underlying 2000–19 files. The charts describe the supplied sample; they do not establish what happened across all marathons or all runners.

For the exact derivation and audit boundaries, see the [reproducible data QA report](https://github.com/adamgreen1708/chart-templates/blob/main/archive/projects/2026-11-marathon-winning-map/data/qa_report.json) and [source-event evidence notes](https://github.com/adamgreen1708/chart-templates/blob/main/archive/projects/2026-11-marathon-winning-map/review/source_event_qa.md).
