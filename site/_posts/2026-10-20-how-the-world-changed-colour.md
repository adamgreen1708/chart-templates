What if a chart lost its axis, its labels and every last number?

Normally, that sounds like a design review going rather badly but in 2018, climate scientist **Ed Hawkins** made it work.

## One year. One stripe.

His *Warming Stripes* turn annual temperatures into a row of coloured bands. Cooler-than-average years are blue; warmer-than-average years move towards red. No degree symbol to decipher. No line to follow.

Read from left to right and the colours change.

The message doesn't need much explaining.

The stripes emerged from Hawkins' work at the University of Reading, with an early version prepared for the **2018 Hay Festival**. He was building on earlier experiments with climate-inspired knitting, rather than claiming to have invented the idea of colouring temperature data.

## The chart with almost nothing on it

There is a clever decision hiding inside all that simplicity, each stripe gets the same amount of room: **one year**. Colour carries the temperature information; the order carries time.

You don't have to know much about charts to see the red gathering on the right. Hawkin successfully packs a lot of communication with very little furniture!

My recreation adds a little back in 🤓

## Why 1961? It depends which stripes.

The colours need a reference average: a period against which warmer and cooler years are judged. **1961 is not a special climate turning point.** It is simply the beginning of one such averaging window.

There's a twist. Hawkins' earlier stripes used **1971–2000**. For the **2023 update**, he changed that to **1961–2010** and adjusted the colour scale to accommodate the unusually extreme temperatures while preserving the familiar look.

My Met Office reconstruction uses a different, longstanding convention: **1961–1990**. None of these choices changes the actual yearly observations, but they do change which temperatures fall either side of the colour midpoint.

## Beautifully clear. Deliberately incomplete.

The stripes hide exact temperatures, degree labels and measurement uncertainty. Blue doesn't mean it was cold everywhere that year; these are **global annual averages**, not local weather reports.

And colour alone needs a verbal explanation for people who can't reliably distinguish the shades.

## TLDR

Hawkins didn't make temperature data less serious. He made the first encounter with it less demanding.

It serves as awonderful way to start a conversation but definitely not quite the last word in it.
