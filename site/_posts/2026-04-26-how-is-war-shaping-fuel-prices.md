<hr />

<p>In late <strong>February 2026</strong>, a geopolitical shock hit global energy markets.</p>

<p>The escalation of the <strong>US–Iran conflict</strong> disrupted one of the world’s most critical oil arteries — the <strong>Strait of Hormuz</strong> — sending <strong>fuel prices</strong> sharply ⬆️ across the globe.</p>

<p>But it doesn’t look like the 😲 has landed evenly.</p>

<p>Some countries saw modest increases. Others experienced extreme spikes. And a few have barely moved at all.</p>

<p>Below isn’t just a story about rising prices, It’s a story about how the global shock wave travelled and where it had been felt most.</p>

<hr />

<h3><strong>How a war is reshaping fuel prices worldwide</strong></h3>

<figure class="story-chart full-bleed"><img src="/assets/migrated/how-is-war-shaping-fuel-prices/img_7845.png" alt="img 7845" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Current (20 Apr) gasoline (petrol) dollar price per litre by % increase (between 23 Feb &#8211; 20 Apr)</figcaption></figure>

<p>If global shocks were simple, you’d expect the most expensive countries to be hit hardest.</p>

<p>But that’s not what happened, Countries with relatively cheap petrol still saw some of the largest percentage increases. Malaysia is a clear example of lower starting prices, but a sharp percentage spike.</p>

<p>Cheap fuel didn’t provide protection. It often came with volatility.</p>

<hr />

<h3>The shock is hitting the real economy through diesel</h3>

<figure class="story-chart full-bleed"><img src="/assets/migrated/how-is-war-shaping-fuel-prices/img_7866.png" alt="IMG 7866" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Gasoline vs Diesel price change % (between 23 Feb – 20 Apr)</figcaption></figure>

<p>The second shift is where the story becomes more serious. In many countries, diesel rose faster than petrol.</p>

<p>That matters because diesel isn’t just another fuel. It powers freight, logistics, agriculture and industry. When diesel prices rise faster, the impact spreads far beyond the pump.</p>

<p>This is how a geopolitical shock becomes an economic one.</p>

<hr />

<h3><strong>Policy may be deciding who feels the shock</strong></h3>

<figure class="story-chart full-bleed"><img src="/assets/migrated/how-is-war-shaping-fuel-prices/img_7881.png" alt="IMG 7881" loading="lazy"><figcaption>A focus on countries around the <strong>zero</strong> zone</figcaption></figure>

<p>While many countries saw sharp increases in both gasoline (Petrol) and/or Diesel, others barely moved at all.</p>

<p>Here pokes 👉🏻 at not just a market story but a policy story.</p>

<p>Subsidies, price controls and government intervention insulate some countries from the global shock 😱, while others absorbed it almost immediately 😭.</p>

<hr />

<h3>What has this meant for the UK?</h3>

<figure class="image-grid">
<figure class="story-chart full-bleed"><img src="/assets/migrated/how-is-war-shaping-fuel-prices/img_7850.png" alt="IMG 7850" loading="lazy"></figure>
</figure>

<p>The UK doesn’t sit at the extreme edge of global fuel prices — but it does sit firmly in the diesel pain zone.</p>

<p>With diesel around $0.45 per litre more expensive than petrol, the UK ranks among the countries where the gap is most pronounced.</p>

<p>That matters because diesel is the backbone of logistics. When diesel carries a premium like this, the impact doesn’t stay at the pump — it flows through supply chains, transport costs and ultimately prices.</p>

<p>In other words, the UK isn’t the most extreme case — but it is exposed in the way that matters most economically.</p>

<h3><br>The takeaway</h3>

<p>This wasn’t just a rise in fuel prices.</p>

<p>It was a global shock — filtered through markets, amplified by diesel, and reshaped by policy.</p>

<p>Cheap fuel hasn’t protect countries. Diesel made the shock worse. And policy decides who feels the pain.</p>
